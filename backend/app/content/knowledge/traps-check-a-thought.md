Thoughts arrive with a feeling of truth attached, even when they are wrong.
"I'm useless" can feel as real as the chair you're sitting on. The skill to
practise is treating the painful ones like claims, not facts.

**Catch it.** Notice the moment your mood drops or tightens, and ask: what
just went through my mind? Write the thought in one sentence, exactly: "I'll
embarrass myself tomorrow and everyone will remember it."

**Check it.** Gently, like a detective, not a prosecutor:

- What is the evidence for it? What is the evidence against?
- Am I in a thinking trap? (Worst case? Mind reading? All or nothing? A
  "should"?)
- What would I tell a friend who said this about themselves?
- What would I think about this on a good day?
- Is there a kinder reading that is still honest?

**Change it — a fairer sentence, not a happy one.** Not "everything will be
wonderful!" Your brain rejects that. Something like: "I'm nervous about
tomorrow. I have prepared, I have got through hard days before, and even if a
moment goes badly, it passes." A fair sentence quiets the storm a little; a
fake one adds the feeling of lying to yourself.

Do it on paper the first few times — thoughts argue better in your head than
on a page. It feels awkward for a week or two, and then it becomes a habit of
mind you carry everywhere.
