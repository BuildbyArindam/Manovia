All-or-nothing thinking sorts the world into two boxes: perfect or ruined,
success or failure, everyone or no-one. There is no room in it for "mostly
fine", which is where most of life actually happens.

It sounds like:

- "If I don't get full marks, I've failed."
- "I missed two days of studying, so the whole week is written off."
- "If they saw the real me, they wouldn't like me."

Why it hooks us: it promises simplicity and high standards. What it delivers
is a system where almost every honest effort counts as failure.

A way to loosen it:

- **Add the middle.** When you catch an all-or-nothing sentence, ask: "What
  would the 70% version of this be?" Seventy percent of the plan, mostly
  rested, mostly kind — that is not failure. That is how good days are built.
- **Use a scale instead of a switch.** From 0 to 10, how did it actually go?
  Most real answers are 3s, 6s and 8s, not 0s and 10s.
- **Watch for the word "completely".** "The week is completely ruined" —
  really? Which parts survived?

The goal is not to lower your standards. It is to let yourself be counted
honestly: a real performance on a real day, which is the only kind anyone
ever has.
